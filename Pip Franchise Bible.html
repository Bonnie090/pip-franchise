Phase 1: Workbook analysis

Source: Trace & Learn with Pip! Lines, Shapes & Letters by Bonnie McCain James. Pencil Control Workbook for Preschool & Kindergarten, ages 3–5, 48 interior pages.

Established from the workbook

Identity

  • Pip is a fox. The back cover says "Join Pip the fox on a colorful tracing adventure!"
  • The workbook doesn't give a gender, pronouns or an age.

Body and proportions

  • Cute chibi style: a very large head, about the size of the whole body. Pip is roughly 2 heads tall.
  • A short, round body with short arms and legs. Pip stands upright on two legs like a person.
  • A big, fluffy tail with a white tip, drawn in pointed, flame-like tufts.

Colors

  • Most of the fur is bright orange.
  • Cream or white on the muzzle, cheeks, chest and belly, the insides of the ears, and the tip of the tail.
  • Dark-brown paws and feet, like socks and mittens.
  • Pink inner ears and pink blush circles on the cheeks.
  • A thick dark-brown outline everywhere, not black.

Face

  • Very large, round, shiny black eyes, each with two white highlights.
  • A small, round, dark-brown nose.
  • The mouth is open in a smile with a pink tongue in every appearance.
  • Small brown eyebrows on the cover and some poses.
  • A small messy tuft of fur on top of the head.
  • Large, pointed ears that stand straight up.

Clothing and accessories

  • A teal or turquoise scarf tied at the neck, with the ends hanging to one side. It's in every pose.
  • No other clothes and no shoes.
  • Often carries a big yellow pencil with a pink eraser, about as tall as Pip. It's on the front cover, the title page and page 47.

Visual style and branding

  • Bright, glossy, cute cartoon style with thick outlines and soft shading.
  • Everyday objects have cute faces: the star, apple, bee, sun, clouds and car.
  • The title uses chunky, rounded, multicolored lettering. "Pip!" is in pink.
  • Setting on the cover: blue sky, fluffy white clouds, a green grassy hill and white daisies. The title page has a rainbow between two smiling clouds.
  • Tracing guides: green "start" dots, dotted lines, arrows and numbered stroke guides.
  • Each section has its own color: lines are coral, shapes are purple, letters are blue, and the opening and closing pages are gold.
  • Many cute animals and objects appear as tracing targets. They are picture icons only, not named characters.

Themes the workbook suggests

  • Learning by practicing, getting a little better each time.
  • Encouragement. The parents' tips page says "Every try is progress."
  • Pride in achievement: the trophy and award page, and Pip celebrating.
  • Adventure, used loosely on the back cover ("colorful tracing adventure").
  • Pip as a friendly guide who waves, points and cheers the child on.
Creative freedom (not established by the workbook)
  • Personality, voice and backstory. (Pip's gender and age have since been decided in Phase 2.)
  • Family, home and friends. There are no supporting characters in the workbook.
  • Where Pip lives. The meadow and sky are only a backdrop.
  • The rules of Pip's world: whether animals or objects talk, and whether magic or imagination plays a part. The smiling objects are a style choice, not a rule.
  • Whether the pencil and the scarf mean anything special.
  • Back and side views, and expressions other than happy. The book only shows Pip happy.
  • The workbook is for ages 3–5. The franchise targets 4–8.
  • A cute orange chibi fox is a common design. Right now the teal scarf is Pip's strongest distinguishing feature. Phase 3 should add one or two more signature details.

Visual elements to keep

  1. He's a fox, with the bright orange and cream color scheme.
  2. Chibi proportions: a big head on a small body, about 2 heads tall.
  3. Large, glossy black eyes with double white highlights.
  4. Pink cheek blush, always visible.
  5. The teal scarf, his strongest signature item.
  6. Dark-brown "sock" paws and feet.
  7. A big fluffy tail with a white tip.
  8. The messy tuft of fur on his head.
  9. Tall pointed ears with pink insides.
  10. Thick dark-brown outlines and the warm, glossy cartoon look.
  11. The big yellow pencil as his recognizable prop.
  12. The brand palette and setting: blue sky, white clouds, green hills, bright rainbow colors, and pink "Pip" lettering.

Phase 2: Pip's character

New creative development

The workbook never establishes a personality, so everything in this phase is new.

The big idea

Most cartoon foxes are sly and sneaky. Pip is the opposite: he's the worst sneaky fox in the world. He's honest, bursting with ideas, and his giant fluffy tail gives away every feeling he tries to hide.

His main flaw comes from the workbook. 📘 The parents' tips page says "Go slowly. Staying near the dots matters more than being fast" and "Every try is progress." Pip is a born rusher. He dreams up a plan, sketches it out, and charges ahead before he's finished drawing it. Learning to slow down, keep trying and accept help is his growth arc.

Core profile

NamePip
SpeciesFox 📘
GenderBoy
Age5 years old. He always insists he's "five-and-a-little-bit."
RoleThe big-idea kid who makes plans for everything

Personality

Pip is bursting with ideas, cheerful, curious and full of energy. He sees a problem, gets excited, pulls out his pencil 📘 and draws a plan. His plans are big, clever and very sincere. They're also missing one important step, usually the one he skipped while rushing. He is honest to a fault, loyal, and quick to bounce back. He also gets loud, and he gets crushed when things go wrong.

Strengths

  • A huge imagination. He comes up with ideas faster than anyone.
  • He's brave about starting things.
  • Completely honest. He literally can't lie, because his tail gives him away.
  • Generous. His plans are usually meant to help someone else.
  • Optimistic. There's always a Plan B, and a Plan C, and so on through the alphabet.

Weaknesses and flaws

These are deliberate, because they drive the comedy and the stories.

  • Impatience. He rushes, skips steps and doesn't read instructions.
  • Overconfidence. "I've got this!" (He doesn't have this.)
  • Doesn't listen. He talks over his friends when he's excited and misses the one useful thing they said.
  • Takes failure hard. When a plan flops he deflates completely, sometimes all at once.
  • Wants to do it all himself. He has trouble asking for help.

Fears

  • Being "too little." He's small, even for a fox, and hates being left out of big-kid things.
  • Letting someone down, especially when a plan meant to help goes wrong.
  • Thunderstorms. Every hair on his tail puffs straight out.
  • Waiting. Not truly a fear, but he acts like it is.

Dreams

  • To invent something so great that everyone uses it and it's named after him.
  • To climb to the very top of the tallest hill and see "everything."
  • To be counted among the big kids.

What drives him

Pip wants to make things better, right now, for his friends, his family and his whole world. His heart is always in the right place. His timing never is.

Likes and dislikes

LikesDrawing plans, sharp pencils, things that go fast, sticky notes, big hills, rainbows after rain, being the first one awake, and anything with wheels.
DislikesWaiting in line, naps ("Naps are for people who don't have plans"), "maybe later," broken pencil tips, and baths (his tail takes forever to dry).
Favorite things to doDrawing "official" plans; building contraptions from junk; racing anyone, anywhere; collecting "useful" stuff; watching clouds and naming their shapes.

Core values

  • Honesty. He always tells the truth, even when it's awkward.
  • Helping others. He wants everyone included.
  • Trying again. He believes every try counts. 📘
  • Friendship over winning, though he learns this one the hard way, more than once.

Sense of humor and running gags

Pip is a physical-comedy kid with big reactions. Some of the jokes are written to land with parents, too.

For kids

  • The tail tells the truth. When Pip tries to keep a secret, his tail twitches, curls or flat-out points at the hidden thing.
  • "I HAVE A PLAN!" He whips out his pencil, sketches fast, and holds up a drawing that's usually just a stick-figure fox and a big arrow.
  • Five-and-a-little-bit. He corrects anyone who just says "five."
  • The puff-up. Fear makes his tail swell to three times its size, and it takes a whole scene to settle back down.

For parents

  • Overly official plan names: "Operation Don't-Get-Wet," "Plan G (for Good This Time)," "The Super-Secret Surprise Party Plan Which Is a Secret." He says the full name out loud, in front of the person it's a secret from.
  • Alphabet plans 📘, tying back to the A–Z pages: "Plan Q? We're at Plan Q?"

How he feels and reacts

  • His feelings are huge and fast. He goes from top joy to total despair and back within one scene.
  • He's empathetic. He notices when someone else is sad, even if he's bad at waiting for them to finish explaining why.
  • After a failure there's a quiet moment: the tail droops and the ears go flat. Then a friend says something kind, or he spots a small win, and he slowly perks back up. That "perk back up" is his signature emotional beat.

Catchphrases

  1. "Ooh! I have a PLAN!" His signature line.
  2. "Pip-pip-HOORAY!" His celebration cheer. 📘 It matches his arms-up victory pose on the award page.
  3. "Okay... Plan B." Said right after a disaster.
  4. "Wait for it... wait for it..." He never waits for it.
  5. "Every try counts!" 📘 The lesson he grows into. Use it sparingly, as an earned moment.

Voice and speech patterns

  • Voice: a bright, quick, slightly raspy little-boy voice. He talks faster as he gets excited, and his voice cracks up high on big moments. An adult voice actor who specializes in kid voices would keep him consistent for years.
  • Voice test: ElevenLabs voice "Elmoe" (silly, energetic, slightly raspy), model Eleven v3, one take of "Ooh! Ooh! I have a PLAN! ... Pip-pip-HOORAY!" (about 104 ElevenLabs credits). Approved: Elmoe is Pip's voice.
  • He narrates his own plans like a sports announcer: "And Pip goes for the ladder..."
  • He measures things in "tail-lengths": "It's like a hundred tail-lengths away!"
  • He starts sentences, gets distracted mid-sentence, then comes back.
  • He asks "Why?" in rapid-fire chains.
  • He speaks simply, like a real 5-year-old, with occasional big words he's proud of (and sometimes uses wrong).

Body language and signature expressions

  • Posture: he leans forward and stands on his toes, as if always about to run.
  • Ears: straight up when he's alert or excited; droop and fold back when he's sad or embarrassed; swivel toward sounds.
  • The "plan face": one eye squints, his tongue pokes out, and the pencil is held up high.
  • The "uh-oh" face: his eyes go wide, his smile freezes into an awkward grin, and his tail tip twitches.
  • The "perk back up": his droopy ears lift one at a time, then his tail does a single slow wag.
  • The victory pose: both arms up and eyes squeezed shut with joy. 📘 Taken directly from the "Great Job!" page.

Core character statement

"Pip is the kind of character who draws a plan for everything, then charges ahead before he's finished drawing it."

Emotional core

"At the heart of Pip is a little fox with a very big heart who wants to make the world better for everyone he loves, and who is learning, one try at a time, that the best things take patience, friends and more than one try."

Design details

FaceA big, soft, round face that is wider than it is tall. The cheeks puff out to the sides in a cream "ruff" with small fluffy points. The cream muzzle area covers the lower half of the face. Pink blush ovals sit just under the outer corners of the eyes.
EyesVery large, round, near-black and glossy, each with one large and one small white highlight in the upper part, both on the same side (the side the light comes from). They sit just below the middle of the head, about one eye-width apart. Each eye is about a quarter of the head's height.
EyebrowsSmall brown arcs floating above the eyes. They're always present in animation because they do much of the acting.

Head tuftThe flame tuft: a bold cluster of soft orange spikes on top of his head, leaning forward, as drawn on the approved model sheet.

Movement style

  • Bouncy and springy. He moves on his toes with lots of squash and stretch, as if he can't keep still.
  • Fast starts. A quick crouch, then he zips off, leaving a small dust puff.
  • Overshoot. He often runs past where he meant to stop and has to backpedal.
  • The tail follows a beat behind every move, and the head tuft and scarf ends bounce, too.
  • Running: arms pumping, ears swept back, tail streaming out behind.
  • Contrast for sad moments: when he's sad, everything slows down and gets heavy. That contrast makes the emotional moments land.

Pose language

  • Forward lean. His body always tilts forward, as if he's about to go somewhere.
  • Clear silhouette. Every pose should read in solid black: ears, tuft, tail and scarf ends are the key shapes.
  • Asymmetry. One ear up and one tilted, one arm doing something, weight on one foot. He rarely stands perfectly straight.
  • Pencil as pointer. He points with his pencil like a conductor's baton when he's explaining a plan.

10 visual features that must never change

  1. Bright orange fur (#FE7617) with a cream muzzle, chest and belly.
  2. Chibi proportions: about 2 heads tall, the head as big as the body.
  3. Large, glossy near-black eyes, each with two white highlights.
  4. Pink blush on his cheeks, always.
  5. The bright teal scarf, tied on his left side.
  6. Chocolate-brown "sock and mitten" paws and feet.
  7. A big fluffy tail with a cream, three-pointed tip.
  8. The spiky, forward-leaning flame tuft on his head.
  9. Tall pointed ears with pink insides.
  10. Thick dark-brown outlines (never black) and the bright, glossy cartoon look.

The pencil and satchel are his signature props, but they aren't on this list: the satchel can come off (bath time, bedtime, swimming), and the pencil only comes out when he's drawing a plan.

How the cast is built

  • Every character shows Pip something he's missing. Pip rushes, so his friends each have a different way of going slow, listening, or asking for help.
  • Different shapes for clear silhouettes. Pip is round with pointy ears and a big tail. Each friend has a different main shape: a dome (Tuck), a spiky ball (Poppy), a teardrop (Skye) and a sturdy rectangle (Max).
  • One signature item each (the Phase 5 rule), each in a color that doesn't clash with Pip's orange and teal.
  • A mix of boys and girls, species, sizes and temperaments, so every child watching finds someone like them.
  • No villains. Even the rival is a friend.

Phase 5: The Pip universe

Built on the workbook

The workbook's backdrop is kept and expanded: blue sky, fluffy white clouds, green rolling hills, white daisies, the rainbow between two smiling clouds, and everyday things with cute faces. The lines, shapes and letters Pip teaches in the workbook become part of the land itself.

New creative development

The workbook gives Pip no home, town, map or world rules, so everything in this phase is new. Who lives in each place is left open for Phase 4 (supporting cast).

The big idea

"Pip lives in a world that looks like it was drawn one line at a time, and he's still learning to draw it."

Pip's valley is shaped by the things he practices in the workbook. 📘 The creek is a wavy line, the far-off mountains are a zigzag, the pond is a perfect circle, and the paths are dotted lines with a green "start" stone at every trailhead. Kids who use the workbook will recognize the world, and kids who watch the show will recognize the workbook.

Where Pip lives: Daisy Dell

Daisy Dell is a small, cozy animal village in a meadow at the bottom of a valley of rolling green hills. 📘 It's the cover setting brought to life: daisies everywhere, a big blue sky, and clouds that drift past close enough to wave at. Homes are tucked into hillsides, tree roots, hollow logs and treetops. Everybody knows everybody, and news travels fast (usually because Pip is shouting it).

The valley is called the Hundred Hills. Pip has not counted them. He has tried.

The rules of Pip's world

RuleHow it works
Animals talkAll the animal characters talk, live in homes, go to school and have families. There are no humans.
The world smiles back 📘The sun, clouds, stars, flowers, apples and other everyday things have cute faces, like in the workbook. They never talk. They only react: blinking, smiling, wincing, yawning or hiding behind each other. It's a quiet comedy layer and makes the world feel alive.
The weather has moodsThe Sun's face and the clouds' moods show the weather. Fluffy white clouds are happy and sleepy. Thunderstorms come from big, grumbly gray Grumbleclouds. They're not villains, they're just grumpy, and they always pass. (Pip is very afraid of them. See Phase 2.)
No magicEverything in the real world works the way it does for kids: things fall, break, get wet and take time. Problems are solved with ideas, effort and friends, never magic.
Imagination is visibleWhen Pip draws a plan, we see it come to life as a "Plan Vision": a short sequence drawn in pencil on paper, with green start dots, dotted lines and arrows, like a workbook page. 📘 The Plan Vision always works perfectly. Then we cut back to reality, where it doesn't.
Simple, handmade technologyWheels, ropes, pulleys, buckets, wagons, kites and string telephones. No screens, phones or computers. Anything Pip invents, a kid could imagine building.
One signature itemLike Pip's scarf, every animal character wears one or two signature items at most (a hat, bow, glasses, apron or bag), not full outfits. This keeps designs simple and readable, and gives each character a merchandise hook.
Gentle stakesThere are no villains. Conflict comes from mistakes, misunderstandings, weather, impatience and feelings. Nobody gets truly hurt, and every episode ends with things a little better than before.
Time stands still (mostly)The seasons change and holidays come and go, but Pip stays five-and-a-little-bit. ("Five-and-a-little-bit-more," he'd say.)

Look and feel of the world

  • Rounded everything. Hills, trees, homes, doors and windows are soft and round. The only sharp shapes are the Zigzag Mountains (far away) and the Grumbleclouds' lightning (rare), which signals that sharp means unusual.
  • Workbook palette. Sky blue (#6DD7FD), grass green (#9FD54C), white clouds and daisies, sunny yellow and rainbow accents. Pip's orange and teal should always be the brightest things on screen.
  • Backgrounds stay softer than characters. Background art uses thinner, colored outlines (dark green, deep blue or warm brown) and slightly softer colors, so the characters, with their thick dark-brown outlines, always pop.
  • Dotted paths. 📘 Footpaths are drawn as dotted trails of pale stepping stones, with a green "start" stone at every trailhead. It's a visual signature of the world and a quiet nod to the workbook.
  • Area colors 📘 borrowed from the workbook sections: Wiggle Creek and the paths lean coral and blue, the Zigzag Mountains are purple, and Rainbow Rise and awards are gold.
  • Light and time of day. Bright mornings for big plans, golden late afternoons for emotional moments, and deep blue nights with smiling stars for bedtime scenes.
  • Tiptop Hill is a hill, not a mountain. A tall, soft, round, green hill with a small cloud resting on top. No snow, no gray rock. The only sharp, purple peaks are the far-off Zigzag Mountains. (Learned from Episode 1, frame 1.)
  • Only Pip's Den has a teal door. His scarf and his front door share the same teal, so no other home uses it. Other doors are warm colors: red, yellow, plum, honey brown. (Learned from Episode 1, frame 1.)

Room to grow

  • Tiptop Hill is the franchise's long-term goal. Pip finally reaching the top and seeing "everything" is a natural season finale or movie.
  • The Zigzag Mountains and beyond leave space for new places, characters and a future big adventure.
  • Whisperwood can hold gentle mysteries and new characters introduced over time.
  • Playsets and products: Pip's Den, Plan HQ and the Great Hill Race racer are natural toy playsets. A "Trace the Map" activity book could teach lines and shapes using the valley's map.

Phase 6: Series name

Built on the workbook

The workbook title, Trace & Learn with Pip!, puts Pip's name at the end with an exclamation point, in pink. 📘 The series name should sit naturally next to it on a shelf, but it stays a separate name (the workbook title is locked as the workbook's only).

New creative development

The series name, tagline and logo direction are all new.

What the name needs to do

  • Say "Pip" clearly, so the workbook and the show build the same brand.
  • Tell a parent what the show is about in a few words.
  • Be easy for a 4-year-old to say and remember.
  • Stretch to books, toys, apps and future workbooks.
  • Be distinctive. "Pip" alone is a common character name in children's media, so the full title has to do the distinguishing.

Phase 7: Story engine

Built on the workbook

The engine runs on the workbook's core idea: go slowly, start at the dot, follow the steps, and every try is progress. 📘 Every episode is a "tracing lesson" in story form.

New creative development

The episode format, story rules, Plan Vision rules and every episode idea are new.

The engine in one line

"Pip has a plan. He skips a step. It goes wrong. With a little help, he goes back to the green dot and tries again."

Format

SeriesPip Has a Plan! Animated comedy for ages 4–8 (fun for parents too).
Episode length11 minutes, two stories per half-hour. This is the standard preschool and early-elementary format.
First season26 half-hours (52 stories).
Short-form"Trace with Pip" 1–2 minute interstitials for streaming and YouTube, where Pip invites kids to trace a line, shape or letter in the air. 📘 These link directly back to the workbook.
Learning goalsSocial-emotional: patience, persistence, listening, asking for help, honesty, handling disappointment. Pre-writing and early learning: lines, shapes, letters, and following steps in order (1, 2, 3...). 📘

Episode structure (11 minutes)

BeatTimeWhat happens
1. The problem0:00–1:30Someone needs help, or Pip wants something right now. Establish the stakes and who it matters to.
2. "Ooh! I have a PLAN!"1:30–2:00Pencil out of the satchel, "plan face," quick sketch. He announces the plan's very official name.
3. The Plan Vision2:00–3:00The plan plays out perfectly in pencil-sketch style (see rules below). One step is missing.
4. Charging ahead3:00–4:30Pip rushes in. A friend tries to warn him ("Um, Pip? What about step four?"). He doesn't listen.
5. Uh-oh4:30–6:30The plan goes wrong, and the comedy escalates. "Okay... Plan B." Plan B makes it worse. Maybe Plan C.
6. The droop6:30–7:30A quiet beat. Tail drops, ears flatten, everything slows down. Pip feels he let someone down.
7. The perk back up7:30–8:15A friend says something kind, or Pip notices a small win or finally listens. One ear lifts, then the other, then one slow tail wag.
8. Back to the green dot8:15–10:00Pip starts over, slowly, with his friends. They do the step he skipped. The solution often ends up different from, and better than, the original plan.
9. Pip-pip-HOORAY!10:00–10:40Success, celebration, the arms-up victory pose. 📘
10. The tag10:40–11:00A final joke, and the plan's sticky note goes up on the Plan Wall at Plan HQ, now with the missing step added.

The Plan Vision

The show's signature sequence and its strongest link to the workbook. 📘

  • Look: the screen becomes a workbook page. Cream paper, gray pencil lines, a green start dot, dotted paths, arrows and numbered steps. Pip and his friends appear as simple pencil sketches.
  • Sound: Pip narrates like a sports announcer ("And Pip goes for the ladder..."), with pencil-scratch sound effects.
  • It always works perfectly. That's the joke.
  • Spot the missing step: the numbered steps visibly skip one (1, 2, 3, 5...). Sharp-eyed kids at home can spot it before Tuck does. This is a built-in "shout at the screen" moment.
  • Transition: an eraser wipes the sketch away and we're back in full color, where reality is about to happen.
  • Once per episode. In the last act the corrected plan can briefly reappear, with the missing step drawn in.

Story rules

AlwaysNever
Pip's heart is in the right place. His plans try to help or include someone.Pip is never mean, sneaky or selfish on purpose.
Things go wrong because of Pip's flaw (rushing, not listening, wanting to do it alone), not just bad luck.No villains. Nobody is out to get anyone.
Pip fixes it himself with his friends. Grown-ups support but don't solve it.Grown-ups never swoop in and fix everything.
Mistakes are funny and normal.Nobody is laughed at or humiliated for failing.
Each friend's strength matters to the solution.A friend is never just the person who says "I told you so."
The lesson is shown through what happens.No lecturing. "Every try counts!" is saved for earned moments, about one story in four.
At least one tail gag per story.No dangerous stunts kids could copy at home (jumping from heights, going in water alone, playing with fire).
End a little better than we started.No sad or scary endings. Every story ends warm.

Season arc: Tiptop Hill

  • Most stories stand alone, so kids can watch in any order.
  • A light thread runs through the season: Pip's dream of reaching the top of Tiptop Hill and seeing "everything." Early on, he can't. In the middle of the season (episode 11), he learns to ask for help.
  • Season finale, "Operation Tiptop": Pip finally makes the climb with the whole gang. He goes slowly, follows Nana's old map step by step, and helps Skye with her biggest flight yet. At the top, "everything" turns out to be Daisy Dell and all the people he loves. A natural special or movie.
  • Pip grows a little across the season (he catches step four himself, once), but his flaw stays, because it's what drives the comedy.

Writer's checklist

  1. Does Pip want to help or include someone?
  2. Is there an official plan name and a Plan Vision with a missing step?
  3. Does it go wrong because of Pip's flaw?
  4. Is there a droop and a perk back up?
  5. Do the friends help solve it, each using their strength?
  6. Do they go back to the green dot and do the missing step?
  7. Is there a tail gag?
  8. Does it end with "Pip-pip-HOORAY!" and the plan on the Plan Wall?
  9. Is the lesson shown, not told?
  10. Is it safe for kids to copy?
